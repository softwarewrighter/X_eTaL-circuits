Experimenting with “A Circuit for In-Context Learning” using local LLMs and OpenCode
The November 3, 2026 paper, A Circuit for In-Context Learning by Andrew Gao and William Gao, is particularly relevant to your work on local coding models, in-context learning (ICL), and eventually fine-tuning a specialized X_eTaL coding assistant.

My recommendation is to reproduce the paper's behavioral experiments first, using OpenCode as the agent and a local LLM as the subject. Then add instrumentation to examine the model's internal mechanisms.

The important distinction is that OpenCode can test whether ICL works, but examining the actual attention heads and residual streams requires access to the model's internals, not merely its inference API.

1. What the paper discovered
The authors investigated how pretrained transformers learn a new classification task from examples in their prompt without updating model weights.

Their central finding is that, for the tasks studied, in-context learning behaves like a gated associative key-value memory implemented by cooperating attention heads.


Conceptual illustration of the paper's associative lookup mechanism, not a trace of actual model activations.
The paper identifies several cooperating components:

Component

Function

Duplicate detectors

Detect whether a label has appeared more than once

Writers

Associate a demonstration's semantic class with its label-token position

Query encoding

Represent the class of the new input

Readers

Match the query against stored class representations and retrieve labels

Gate

Enable or suppress associative retrieval

Brakes

Counteract bias toward the most recently observed label

Copiers/readout

Convert retrieved information into output-token probabilities

These are not just proposed abstractions. The researchers used activation patching, attention-head ablation, and controlled steering to establish causal evidence for the components.

Why this matters for your coding agents
Your X_eTaL coder needs to learn from context containing language documentation, examples, idioms, compiler errors, and corrections.

A potentially important finding
The paper reports that some apparently simple features, such as certain word-position and digit rules, fail to become usable class representations in the studied models. In some cases, intervening on the internal representation improves performance, suggesting a bottleneck in constructing the key rather than retrieving it.

For X_eTaL, that suggests testing whether failures arise from:

Failure to understand the relevant language construct.

Failure to associate the construct with the demonstrated solution.

Failure to retrieve the correct association.

Failure to generate syntactically valid code after retrieval.

what is this concept "circuits" and how does it apply to how inference works in general?
What are circuits in LLMs, and how do they relate to inference?
A circuit in a neural network is a collection of neurons, attention heads, and other computational components that cooperate to perform a particular function.

The idea comes from mechanistic interpretability: researchers are trying to reverse-engineer neural networks to understand how they actually compute their answers.

A useful analogy is an electronic circuit. A CPU contains transistors, gates, registers, and arithmetic units. Those components cooperate to implement operations such as addition, comparison, and branching.

An LLM has a very different architecture, but researchers are discovering computational structures that perform recognizable operations such as:

Recognizing repeated tokens.

Identifying relationships between words.

Copying information from earlier context.

Associating labels with concepts.

Retrieving information from previous examples.

Influencing which token gets generated next.

The important difference is that LLM circuits are generally learned, distributed, overlapping, and not explicitly designed by programmers.

1. From transformer architecture to circuits
You already know that a transformer consists of layers containing attention and feed-forward networks.

The conventional architecture looks something like this:

This tells us the architecture, but not what the model learned to do with it.

Circuit analysis asks a different question:

Which specific components and information pathways actually cause a particular behavior?

For example, consider a model completing:

Paris is the capital of France. Berlin is the capital of

A circuit-level investigation might discover that certain components identify the country-city relationship, others retrieve or represent relevant geographic information, and still others contribute to the final prediction.

That is a hypothetical decomposition, not an established circuit for this exact example.

2. An example: induction heads
One of the best-studied circuits in transformers involves induction heads.

Consider this input:

A B C D ... A B

A model may predict C, because it recognizes that the sequence A B previously occurred and was followed by C.

A simplified induction circuit works like this:


Simplified conceptual illustration of induction, not an exact attention pattern.
In the classic two-head account:

A previous-token head makes information about the preceding token available.

An induction head finds an earlier matching context and copies information about the token that followed it.

The remarkable thing is that this behavior can emerge during training without anyone explicitly programming a sequence-matching algorithm.

Induction heads were an important early result because researchers could identify a computational mechanism and experimentally test its causal role.

4. How circuits operate during inference
Consider the sequence:

Prompt → prefill → decode → output

During prefill, the transformer processes the prompt and computes representations for its tokens. During decoding, it uses the cached context representations and computes the next token.

Circuits can operate in both stages.

Stage

What happens

Possible circuit activity

Token embedding

Tokens become vectors

Encode useful features

Early layers

Local and contextual representations form

Detect token patterns and relationships

Middle layers

Representations are transformed and combined

The early/middle/late distinctions are broad tendencies, not rigid boundaries. A particular circuit may span many layers.

Also, circuits do not necessarily operate once per prompt. They can contribute repeatedly as the model generates each new token.

Circuits versus KV cache
These are related but different concepts.

The inference KV cache stores previously computed key and value tensors so that autoregressive decoding does not need to recompute the entire prefix.

A circuit is a computational mechanism that may use those tensors.

For example, an induction head can use cached keys and values to locate an earlier pattern and retrieve information.

The cache provides the data; the learned attention operations determine how it is used.

6. Are circuits the same as neurons, attention heads, or MoE experts?
No. These are different levels of abstraction.

Concept

What it represents

Neuron

A scalar activation or computational unit

Feature

A meaningful property represented in activations, often distributed

Attention head

A learned attention computation

MLP

A learned nonlinear transformation

MoE expert

One of several selectable feed-forward subnetworks

Circuit

A cooperating collection of components and information pathways

Model

The complete network containing many interacting mechanisms

A circuit might include portions of several attention heads, features distributed across residual streams, and MLP computations.

Likewise, one attention head can participate in multiple circuits.

An MoE expert is not necessarily a circuit. It is an architectural module selected by a router. A circuit may involve multiple experts and attention heads, and different inputs may activate different pathways.

This matters for your interest in MoE coding models: expert routing and circuit activation are not equivalent.

9. The deeper implication: LLMs may contain learned algorithms
The most interesting aspect of circuit research is that it connects neural networks to classical computer science.

A circuit can sometimes be described in algorithmic terms:

Compare two representations.

Detect equality or similarity.

Copy a value.

Associate a key with a value.

Retrieve a value using a query.

Suppress an incorrect candidate.

Combine evidence.

These are familiar operations from conventional programming.

But unlike conventional programs, neural networks usually implement them approximately, through continuous-valued vector operations, and often with overlapping representations.

The goal of mechanistic interpretability is to discover which algorithms have emerged and how they are implemented.

The central takeaway: transformer inference is the execution of a fixed learned numerical computation. Circuits are the functional mechanisms researchers discover inside that computation. They help explain why particular outputs arise, not merely how matrix multiplications are performed.

For your research, I see two complementary directions: use circuit analysis to understand why a local coding model succeeds or fails at ICL, and build small educational models where you can observe circuits emerging during training. The latter could be particularly valuable in X_eTaL-ML because it connects mathematical tensor operations directly to recognizable computational algorithms.

in the ICL paper, how did the authors discover or reason about circuits?
How the authors discovered the ICL circuit
The most interesting aspect of A Circuit for In-Context Learning is that the authors did not simply inspect attention heads and assign them functions. They approached the transformer like engineers reverse-engineering an unknown machine.

Their methodology was essentially:

Observe a behavior → construct a hypothesis → isolate a variable → intervene inside the model → measure the causal effect → assemble a circuit → test whether the circuit explains the original behavior.

That is a particularly useful methodology for your X_eTaL research because much of it can be expressed as tensor experiments, controlled transformations, and statistical analysis.

1. They started with a behavior they could precisely measure
Rather than investigate general intelligence or unrestricted conversation, they chose a narrow task: few-shot classification with arbitrary labels.

For example:

The food was delicious -> foo
The service was terrible -> bar
A wonderful evening -> foo
The soup was cold -> bar

I would never return ->
The correct answer is bar.

Why use invented labels?

Because the model cannot rely on a previously learned association between the word bar and negative sentiment. It must infer the mapping from the prompt.

3. They used activation patching to locate information
This is the crucial step.

Suppose they run both prompts through the model and record internal activations.

At some layer, they replace the activation from the original prompt with the corresponding activation from the modified prompt.

They then continue inference.

If the model's answer changes toward the modified prompt's answer, the patched activation carries causally relevant information.


Activation patching: transplant a selected activation between runs and measure its effect on the prediction.
They performed these experiments at different layers, attention heads, token positions, and key/value projections.

This allowed them to answer questions such as:

Where is the demonstrated label stored?

Where is the class information stored?

Which heads transfer class information into the label position?

Which heads retrieve the correct label?

An important methodological distinction: finding a large patching effect identifies a causal pathway under that intervention, but does not automatically prove the patched component is the unique or complete representation of the information.

They tested this hypothesis using two different interventions.

This is how they established that the residual stream at each label position behaves like a memory slot containing both a class key and a label value.

They did not merely observe that attention heads look at label tokens. They showed that the two sides of the attention computation carry different information.

5. They worked backward and forward through the computation
Once they found the apparent memory slots, they asked two questions:

Who writes the keys? They traced class information backward to middle-layer attention heads that attend from a label token to its preceding sentence. Roughly twenty heads contributed to writing the class representation.

Who reads the keys? They traced information forward to upper-layer attention heads that match the query against the label positions and retrieve the associated label.

They then discovered two additional mechanisms.

The first was a gate: early duplicate-token detectors and subsequent pooling heads produce a signal indicating whether labels have repeated. That signal influences whether readers attend to the memory slots or primarily to an attention sink at position zero.

The second was a population of brake heads that counteracts a bias toward the most recently seen label.

The gate was particularly revealing. They found a residual-stream direction associated with repeated labels, added that direction to another run, and observed that it changed the readers' attention to the sink. Random directions with the same magnitude did not produce comparable effects. 

arXiv

This is an important methodological principle: a hypothesized mechanism becomes much more convincing when deliberately manipulating its signal produces the predicted downstream effect.

They kept selected heads active and replaced the outputs of other heads with reference activations.

There is an important qualification: these isolation experiments were performed at the query position, with the demonstration memory slots constructed normally. They therefore tested the sufficiency of the readout circuitry more directly than the complete end-to-end construction of the memory.

8. They checked whether the mechanism generalized
Finally, they investigated whether they had discovered a Qwen-specific accident or a more general transformer algorithm.

They repeated important interventions in SmolLM3-3B and Llama-3.2-3B.

They found similar functional roles, but the specific heads and layers differed.

They also inspected training checkpoints of OLMo-2 models. The components appeared in a meaningful sequence: repeated-label copying first, the null behavior next, and class-selective retrieval later.

That supports the interpretation that the circuit is a learned computational mechanism rather than a coincidental attention pattern.

How can I relate this to BDH (Baby Dragon Hatclings) in that it is designed to be introspectable?
Connecting ICL circuits to BDH's introspectable architecture
The connection between Baby Dragon Hatchling (BDH) and the recent A Circuit for In-Context Learning paper is potentially quite important for your X_eTaL research.

They approach the same fundamental problem from opposite directions:

Mechanistic interpretability of transformers: Start with a trained, largely opaque neural network and reverse-engineer the computational mechanisms it learned.

BDH: Design a neural architecture whose internal computational structure is intended to be easier to inspect, trace, and interpret from the beginning.

This suggests a useful research question:

Can we use the experimental methodology from the ICL circuit paper to discover functional circuits in BDH, and determine whether BDH makes those circuits easier to understand than equivalent transformer circuits?

The important distinction is between observability and interpretability. Having access to individual neuron activations does not automatically mean we understand what the neurons compute, why they activate, or whether they cause a particular behavior.

A rigorous comparison would examine both the internal representation and its causal role.

2. What makes BDH especially interesting?
The original Dragon Hatchling paper describes several properties specifically intended to support interpretability:

Sparse, nonnegative activations: Relatively few neurons are active for a given input, making activity patterns easier to inspect.

Concept-associated neurons and synapses: The authors report examples of monosemantic representations, where particular neurons or connections respond to recognizable concepts.

Hebbian working memory: Information encountered during inference can alter a dynamic synaptic state, rather than being represented exclusively through a token-indexed KV cache.

Graph-based organization: The model can be interpreted as a network of interacting neurons and synapses, providing a natural substrate for visualizing computational pathways.

These are claims and experimental findings from the BDH research, not proof that every BDH computation is transparently understandable.

8. A particularly interesting extension: ICL versus synaptic learning
There is another distinction worth investigating.

In conventional transformer ICL, the pretrained weights generally remain fixed, and the model adapts through context-dependent activations and its KV cache.

In BDH, dynamic synaptic state is part of the inference computation.

That gives you an opportunity to study two kinds of adaptation:

Transformer: learning-like behavior through contextual representations.

BDH: learning-like behavior through dynamic synaptic state.

Neither necessarily implies persistent updates to pretrained parameters.

You could run a sequence of examples, inspect how the BDH synaptic state changes, reset it, replay the examples, and test whether particular changes are necessary for later retrieval.

That is closely related to your interests in ICL, ICRL, and alternatives to conventional fine-tuning.

It could also help distinguish short-term associative memory from longer-term learning.
